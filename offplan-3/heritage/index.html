العمارة النجدية

Built for heat, built to last.

Najdi builders worked with mud, palm and stone for five centuries. This is what they knew, and what we took from it.

Six centuries on
the Wadi Hanifah.

From the first settlement at Diriyah to the district we are building a few kilometres downstream.

  2. 1446

    Diriyah is founded

    Mani' Al-Muraydi settles on the banks of Wadi Hanifah. Houses rise in sun-dried mud brick around small private courts, a pattern that will hold for 500 years.

  3. 1727

    A capital in mud brick

    Diriyah becomes the capital of the first Saudi state. The At-Turaif quarter fills with palaces, mosques and family houses, all crowned with triangular parapets.

  4. 1818

    Siege and a new capital

    Diriyah falls after a long siege. In 1824 the capital moves a few kilometres down the wadi to Riyadh, built with the same mud, palm and stone.

  5. 1950s

    Concrete arrives

    Air-conditioned villas replace courtyard houses across Riyadh. Homes turn outward to the street, and the shaded court all but disappears.

  6. 2010

    At-Turaif joins the World Heritage List

    UNESCO inscribes the At-Turaif district. Restoration brings back the craft of mud plaster and athl timber, and a new generation of builders learns it.

  7. 2021

    Identity in new buildings

    The King Salman Charter for Architecture and Urbanism asks new projects to draw on local character. Najdi style becomes a reference for Riyadh again.

  8. 2028

    Mirath hands over

    212 homes in eight courtyard buildings, using the old rules with modern structure, glazing and cooling.

How to read
a Najdi wall.

Eight details on one facade. Each has a job; none is only decoration. Hover or tap a point to find it.

What we kept,
what we changed.

Copying an old house brick for brick would give you its problems too. We kept the ideas and rebuilt the means.

Najdi houseMirath

The parapet

Mud shurfat, re-plastered after each rainy season, about 1.5 m high around a roof the family slept on in summer.

Glass-fibre concrete shurfat with a lime finish, 1.8 m high, around terraces with drainage and power. No re-plastering, same silhouette.

The opening

Small holes in thick walls, closed with timber shutters. Cool, private, and quite dark inside.

Taller slot windows set 30 cm deep in the wall, with low-e double glazing. Twice the daylight of a Najdi room, still no direct summer sun on the glass.

The cooling

Courts, thick walls and, in the Gulf towns, wind towers moved air through the house without power.

The court and thick walls stay; quiet VRF units and heat-recovery fresh air do the rest, with a cooling load about 27% below a standard flat.

A library of doors.

Reference doors from our design library. Mirath's entrance doors are being drawn with Riyadh carpenters in athl timber, each building with its own painted pattern.

The craft stays in the hands that know it.

Lime plaster on the courtyard walls is applied by teams trained on heritage restoration in the region, not sprayed by machine. Shutters and doors are made in a Riyadh workshop. It costs more, and it is the part of Mirath you will touch every day.
